2. **assistant**: You're not missing anything obvious. A list doesn't "show" anything by itself. It becomes evidence when you ask what it was written to do and then look at patterns in it. Unit 5 of the Reader gives you two ways to read a ledger:

- **Down the page:** who appears, how often, and for what.
- **Across time:** where entries thin out or stop, and what else might explain that.

It also asks you to keep three things apart: what the record was *for*, what it *shows* about discipline, and what it's *silent* about.

Here's a parallel example on made-up material, not your ledger. Imagine a page from a 1950s cannery's tardy book:

> Mar 3, Lopes, packing, 10 min. Mar 3, Hart, labeling, 5 min. Mar 4, Lopes, packing, 15 min. Mar 6, Lopes, packing, 10 min. Mar 9-14: no entries, "line down."

- **Down the page:** Lopes shows up three times and everyone else once. So the rule was enforced, and perhaps enforced unevenly, or Lopes really was late a lot. Even 5 minutes got recorded, so the rule was strict.
- **Across time:** The entries stop March 9-14, but the note says the line was down. Silence here doesn't mean everyone was punctual. It means nobody was working.
- **Silent about:** whether other workers were late and not recorded, why Lopes was late, and what the workers thought of the rule.

Notice how each claim comes from a specific detail, and how I separated "what the page shows" from "what I can't know."

Now try it on your own page. Pick one pattern from the ledger, such as a repeated name, a type of offence, the size of the amounts, or the gap near the end of the month.
