2. **assistant**: You're not missing anything obvious. A list *is* just names and amounts, and the trick is learning what to ask of it. Reader unit 5 says administrative records weren't written to tell a story, and that's what makes them useful. Here's a parallel example on different material.

**Imagine a school detention book:**
> Oct 3, Marsh, late, 1 detention. Oct 4, Pike, talking in class, 1 detention. Oct 9, Marsh, late, 2 detentions. *Oct 14-18: no entries (note: "school closed, snow").*

Even this bare list tells us a few things:
- **What rules were enforced:** lateness and talking were punishable.
- **Who got caught, and how often:** Marsh appears twice, and the penalty went up the second time.
- **Where the entries stop:** a blank week isn't necessarily a week of perfect behavior. The note says nobody was at school.

It can't tell us who was late without being noticed, which offenses a teacher let slide, or what Marsh thought of the detentions. The ledger records only what a particular person was required to write down.

The Reader suggests reading a ledger in two directions: *down the page* (who, how often, for what) and *across time* (where entries thin out or stop, and what else was happening then). It also says to keep three things apart: what the record was for, what it shows, and what it's silent about.

Before you apply that to excerpt E, here's one question to start from: **who wrote this ledger, for whom, and what would that person have been required to record?**
