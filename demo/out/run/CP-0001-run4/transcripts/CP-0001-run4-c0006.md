**The idea:** a fines ledger is a record of rules being enforced. Each entry tells you three things: a rule existed (you can't be fined for something that isn't prohibited), someone was willing to enforce it, and a price was attached. Patterns across entries show more: who gets fined, how often, for what, and when. The Reader also says to keep three things apart: what the record was *for*, what it *shows* about discipline, and what it's *silent* about.

**Worked example (invented, not your assignment):** Imagine a page from a mill's fines book, March 1890:

- Mar 3, Hale, weaving room, talking, 10c
- Mar 4, Hale, weaving room, talking, 10c
- Mar 6, Voss, spinning room, late, 25c
- Mar 10, Hale, weaving room, late, 25c
- Mar 11-14: no entries (marginal note: "water low, mill stopped")
- Mar 17, Voss, spinning room, broken bobbin, 50c

Reading it **down the page**: talking and lateness are punishable, and so is damaging equipment, at a higher price. Hale appears three times, so either Hale broke rules more than others or Hale was watched more closely. The ledger alone can't tell us which.

Reading it **across time**: the gap March 11-14 isn't "good behavior." The note explains it: nobody was fined because nobody was working.

**What it's silent about:** whether other workers talked without being caught, whether the overseer let things slide, and what Hale thought of being fined.

That's the whole move: turn each feature of the page into a claim, and be honest about where the evidence stops.
