**Parallel example (not your source):** Imagine a school's detention book for one week: date, student, teacher, reason, minutes. You could ask it two kinds of questions:

- **Down the page:** Which reasons come up again and again? Do the same few names recur? That points to which rules the school actually enforced and who got caught under them. This is **evidence**, because it's written right there.
- **Across time:** Suppose the entries stop on Wednesday and Thursday. Is that because everyone behaved? Or was the school closed for a snow day? You'd have to work that out from other clues. That's **inference**.

Unit 5 of the Primary Source Reader also stresses what the book *can't* show: misbehaviour no teacher noticed, offences let off with a warning, and what the students thought of it. A good analysis keeps three things apart: what the record was for, what it shows, and what it's silent about.

Unit 5 calls this reading in two directions (down the page, and across time), and it treats a silence as a fact to be explained rather than a gap.
