2. **assistant**: That's a common sticking point, and it usually means the task is about *what kind of question* to ask. The worksheet doesn't ask you to summarize the letter. It asks you to treat the letter as evidence and say what it's good for and what it isn't. Reader unit 3 gives a method with four moves:

1. Name the author's position and interest: who wrote it, to whom, and why.
2. List what the author could see from that position and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available.
4. State what the document can show and what it cannot.

Here is the method on a parallel example, not your source. Imagine a mill owner's letter to his investors saying that "agitators" pulled "loyal hands" off the looms.

- **Position and interest:** The owner is writing to people who control his money, so he wants to look in control.
- **What he could and couldn't see:** He could see the empty mill and the lost output. He couldn't see what was said in workers' meetings or why individuals walked out.
- **Word choice:** "Agitators" and "loyal hands" could have been "organizers" and "workers."
- **Can show:** How an owner framed a strike to investors, and which names he bothered to record.
- **Cannot show:** Why the workers actually struck, or how many really wanted to.

You'd do the same kind of thinking with Pruitt's letter, using your own evidence from it.
