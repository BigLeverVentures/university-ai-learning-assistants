2. **assistant**: That's a common place to get stuck, so here's how the worksheet entry is built. Reader unit 3 frames it as a two-column list written in prose: what the source can show, and what it can't. You get there by asking a few questions of the source in order:

1. **Position and interest:** who wrote it, to whom, and what might they want that reader to believe or do?
2. **Vantage point:** what could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choice:** where did the author pick one word when others were available?
4. **Can show / cannot show:** what does it reveal (the author's view, the language of authority, whose names got recorded), and what can't it tell you (other people's motives, events the author didn't witness)?

Here's a parallel example on made-up material. Imagine a mill owner's letter to his board of directors says, "Agitators from outside misled my workers into leaving." It shows how the owner wanted the board to see the strike, and it uses the language of authority ("agitators," "misled"). It can't show why the workers actually walked out, because he's describing their motives without having asked them. Notice that I didn't ask whether the owner was right. I asked what his position let him see and what he had reason to say.
