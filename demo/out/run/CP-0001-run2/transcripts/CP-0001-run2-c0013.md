**The method in short**
1. **Position and interest:** Who wrote it, to whom, and what did they want from the reader?
2. **Vantage point:** What could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choice:** Where did the author pick one word when others were available?
4. **Can show / cannot show:** What does the document reveal (the author's view, the language of authority, whom they bothered to name), and what can it not tell you (other people's motives, events the author didn't witness)?

Your 150 words are basically that last step, written in prose and backed by the first three.

**Worked example (not your source)**
Imagine an invented note from a ferry clerk to his manager: *"Passengers made a disturbance at the landing over the new fare. Most were willing to pay, but a few troublemakers, chiefly a carter named Voss, stirred up the rest. I turned no one away."*

- **Position:** A clerk reporting upward, probably wanting to look in control.
- **Vantage:** He saw the landing, but not what was said in the crowd beforehand, or why people were upset.
- **Words:** "Disturbance" and "troublemakers" where "protest" or "objectors" were possible.
- **Shows:** How a clerk framed the dispute for his boss, and that he knew Voss by name and trade. **Cannot show:** why passengers objected, or whether most really were "willing."

Written as prose, that becomes a short evaluation: perspective first, then what it shows, then what it can't.
