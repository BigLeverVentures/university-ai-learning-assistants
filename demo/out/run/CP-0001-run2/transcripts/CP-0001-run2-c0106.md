**The steps (from Reader unit 3)**
1. Name the author's position and interest: who wrote it, to whom, and what they wanted.
2. List what the author could see from that position and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available.
4. State what the document can show and what it cannot.

**A worked example on a different, invented source**

Suppose a ship's purser writes to the shipping company: *"The crew refused duty on the 9th. The mate lost control of the deck. I believe most were led astray by two troublemakers. The cargo is undamaged."*

1. **Position and interest:** The purser is a company officer reporting upward, so he has reason to show he did his job and to put blame elsewhere.
2. **Seen and unseen:** He saw the deck and the cargo. He could not see the crew's private conversations or their grievances.
3. **Word choices:** "Refused duty" and "led astray" frame the crew as disobedient followers, where "protested" or "chose" would have framed them differently.
4. **Can and cannot show:** It can show the company's view, the language officers used about crews, and which two names were singled out. It cannot show why the crew acted, or whether most really were "led astray."

Written as prose, that becomes a short paragraph covering perspective, what it shows, and what it can't.

6. **assistant**: Yes, it matters a lot. Audience affects what a writer includes, what they leave out, and how they describe things. That's why the Reader tells you to start with the author's position and interest.

**Why audience changes the document**
- **What gets included:** A writer tells a reader what that reader cares about. A superintendent cares about whether the shops and engines are secure and who is to blame, so a report to him will cover those things.
- **What gets framed:** The writer knows the reader has power over him. That pushes him toward showing he acted properly and put responsibility elsewhere.
- **Word choice:** Words get picked to fit the reader's expectations. This is the Reader's "ringleaders" versus "spokesmen" point. The word you choose tells the reader whose side you're on.

So the audience tells you what the document can't be trusted to say, and also what it reveals about how people spoke to those in authority.

**What your ~150 words should contain**
The prompt asks for three things, and the Reader says to write them as prose rather than as a list:
1. **Perspective:** who wrote it, to whom, and what interest that gave them (about 40-50 words).
2. **What it can show:** what the author's position let them see, and what the document reveals about their view and language (about 50 words).
3. **What it cannot show:** what was out of the author's view, or what he can't speak to reliably (about 50 words).

Tie each claim to something specific in the letter, such as a word choice or a detail, so it's evidence rather than a general statement. The Reader's own contrasts (like "ringleaders" versus "spokesmen") show the kind of detail that works.
