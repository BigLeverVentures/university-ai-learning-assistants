**The steps**
1. **Position and interest:** Who wrote it, to whom, and what did they want from the reader?
2. **Vantage point:** What could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choices:** Where did the author pick a loaded word when a neutral one was available?
4. **Can and cannot show:** Based on 1-3, what does the document reliably reveal (the author's view, the language of authority, whom they bothered to name), and what can't it tell you (other people's motives, events the author didn't witness)?

**A worked example on different material (invented, not your assignment)**
Imagine a harbor master's report to the port board after dockworkers stopped unloading a ship: *"The gang walked off the wharf at noon. Agitators from the saloon had been working on them. The cargo is secure. I ask the board to back me in refusing any terms."*

- *Position:* He's a manager reporting to his own employers, and he wants backing.
- *Vantage:* He saw the walkout at the wharf, but not the saloon conversations. "Agitators" is a claim about things he didn't witness.
- *Words:* "Agitators" suggests outsiders stirring things up, where "organizers" or "friends" would suggest something else.
- *Can/cannot:* It shows how a manager framed a walkout to his superiors. It can't show why the dockworkers actually left.

Notice that each point comes from something specific in the text, and the entry ends up being a short argument, not a list of facts.

6. **assistant**: Yes, it matters a lot, because audience shapes almost every choice a writer makes. The Reader says this in its first lines: every document was made by someone, *for someone*, *to do something*. The audience decides:

- **What gets included.** Writers put in what the reader needs or what helps their case.
- **What gets left out.** Writers skip what the reader already knows or what would reflect badly on them.
- **How things are worded.** The same event gets different words depending on who is reading.
- **What the writer wants the reader to think of the writer.** A report to a boss is partly a performance of competence and loyalty.

**What the worksheet asks for (about 150 words)**
Three things, written as connected prose rather than a list:

1. **Whose perspective it gives.** Name the author, his position, and who he's writing to. Add what he wants from that reader. (About 40-50 words.)
2. **What it can show.** Say what the letter reveals, using the Reader's examples: the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, the names that were recorded. Tie each claim to a specific word or line, quoted briefly. (About 50-60 words.)
3. **What it cannot show.** Say what's out of view or can't be known from this source, such as other people's motives or events Pruitt didn't witness. Again, point to something in the text. (About 40-50 words.)
