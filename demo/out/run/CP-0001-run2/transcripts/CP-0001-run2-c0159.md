**Practice example (made up for illustration)**

> *"To the Mill Committee, 3 March 1890. The weavers walked out Tuesday after a few agitators spread false stories about wage cuts. Most of our people are loyal and want to return. I have kept the looms oiled and the gates secured. -- J. Alden, Overseer"*

**Walking through the method:**

1. **Position and interest.** Alden is a mid-level manager writing upward to the people who employ him. He has a stake in looking competent and in showing the walkout wasn't his fault.
2. **What he could and couldn't see.** He could see the mill floor, who was at their looms, and who left. He probably couldn't see the weavers' private conversations or their reasons.
3. **Word choice.** He says "agitators" and "false stories," where "organizers" or "disputed claims" were available. He also says "loyal" without saying how he knows.
4. **Can show / can't show.** It can show how a manager framed a strike for his bosses and what vocabulary he used to discredit it. It can't show why the weavers left, whether the wage cuts were real, or what most workers actually wanted. Those are his claims, not evidence.

Notice that the last step separates what's in the document from what the author *asserts*. That distinction is the heart of "what it can and can't show."
