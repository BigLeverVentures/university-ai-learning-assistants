Reader unit 3 calls it **reading against the grain**: the letter tells you what Pruitt wants his superintendent to believe, but it also shows things he didn't mean to show. Your entry sorts these into two columns, written as prose: what the source *can* show and what it *can't*.

Here's the method on a parallel example, not your letter. Imagine a principal's 1890 report to the school board about a student walkout:

> "A few troublemakers, led by Tom Ames, persuaded the pupils to leave at noon. I do not believe one child in five wished to go. I kept the building open and the teachers at their posts."

1. **Position and interest:** The principal is writing to his bosses and wants to look in control.
2. **Could see / couldn't see:** He saw the building and the teachers. He wasn't in the students' conversations, so he can't know why they left.
3. **Word choices:** "Troublemakers" rather than "organizers." That signals how he sees challengers to authority.
4. **Can show:** the principal's view, the language of authority, and that he knew Tom Ames by name. **Can't show:** the students' real motives, or how many truly wanted to leave. "One in five" is his guess.

Notice that a claim like "most didn't want to go" is evidence of what he *wanted the board to think*, not of what the students felt.
