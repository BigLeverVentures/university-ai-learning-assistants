1. **student**: I have to evaluate this letter from the foreman for my worksheet. I read it twice and I don't get what I'm supposed to say about it.
2. **assistant**: That's a common sticking point, so here's a way into it. The Reader's unit 3 method turns "evaluate a source" into a short checklist:

1. **Position and interest:** who wrote it, to whom, and what do they want the reader to believe or do?
2. **Could see / couldn't see:** what was in view from the author's position, and what wasn't?
3. **Word choices:** places where the author picked one word when others were available.
4. **Can show / cannot show:** what the document reveals (the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, whose names got recorded) versus what it can't (other people's motives, events the author didn't witness).

Here's a **practice example on made-up material, not your assignment.** Imagine a mill owner's letter to his investors saying, "A handful of agitators stirred up my loyal hands, who were content until outsiders arrived."

- *Position and interest:* He's writing to people who control his money, so he wants to look like he's in command and the trouble came from outside.
- *Could see / couldn't see:* He could see the mill gates and the balance sheets, but not the workers' kitchen-table conversations.
- *Word choices:* "Agitators" instead of "organizers," and "loyal hands" instead of "employees."
- *Can show:* How an owner framed a strike for investors. *Cannot show:* Whether the workers were actually content, or why they walked out.

Notice that none of that is a verdict like "biased, so useless." It's a description of what the source is good for and what it isn't.
