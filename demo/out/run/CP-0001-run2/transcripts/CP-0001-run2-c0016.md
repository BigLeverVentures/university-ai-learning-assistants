**The method in four moves**
1. **Position and interest:** Who wrote this, to whom, and what did they want from the reader?
2. **Vantage point:** What could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choice:** Where did the author pick a word when other words were available?
4. **Can show / cannot show:** What does the document reveal (the author's view, the language of authority, whose names were recorded)? What can't it tell you (other people's motives, events the author didn't witness)?

Your worksheet is those moves written as a short prose paragraph.

**Worked example on parallel material (invented, not your assignment)**
Imagine a landlord's letter to the city council in 1910 about a rent protest in his buildings. It says, "A mob gathered in the courtyard, led by a tailor named Weiss, and I believe most tenants were frightened into joining."

- *Position:* He's a property owner writing to officials, and he wants action against the protest.
- *Vantage:* He may have watched the courtyard, but he wasn't in the tenants' private conversations.
- *Words:* He says "mob" where "gathering" was available, and "frightened into joining" where "chose to join" was possible.
- *Shows / can't show:* It shows how an owner described protest to officials and that he knew Weiss by name. It can't show why tenants joined or whether they were frightened.

In prose, that becomes something like: "This letter gives the landlord's perspective, written to persuade officials. It can show..., but it cannot show..."

6. **assistant**: Yes, it matters a lot. Audience shapes what a writer includes, how they describe it, and what they leave out. Reader unit 3 puts it this way: "Every document was made by someone, for someone, to do something."

**Why audience changes the document**
- **It shapes what gets said.** A writer tells a reader what that reader needs to know, or what the writer wants them to believe. The same events would read differently in a private letter to a friend or in a report to a boss.
- **It shapes the words.** Writers choose language that fits their relationship to the reader. A subordinate writing to a superior often uses the vocabulary of authority and stresses their own reliability.
- **It gives the writer a stake.** If the reader has power over the writer, such as power to promote, blame, or dismiss, the writer has a reason to present events in a flattering light.

**Using the landlord example again**
The landlord wrote to city officials who could send police or enforce rules. So "mob" and "frightened into joining" aren't neutral descriptions. They steer officials toward action. If he'd written to a tenant friend, he might have described the same courtyard scene very differently. That doesn't make his letter useless. It means the letter shows how he wanted officials to see the protest, which is real evidence, but about his view rather than the tenants' reasons.

**What this means for you**
Audience is the bridge between move 1 (position and interest) and the rest of the method. Once you know who the letter was for and what the writer wanted from that reader, you can read the word choices, the details included, and the gaps with that purpose in mind.
