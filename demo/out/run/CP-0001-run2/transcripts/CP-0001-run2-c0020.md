**The method in four moves**
1. **Name the author's position and interest.** Who is writing, to whom, and what do they want?
2. **Sort what they could see from what was out of view.** Where were they standing, literally and socially?
3. **Notice word choices.** Where did the author pick one word when another was available?
4. **State what the document can show and what it can't.** The Reader says to write this as a two-column list, but in prose.

**Worked example (invented, not from your packet)**
Imagine a ferry captain's report to the owner: *"The crew refused to cast off at dawn. I believe the cook put them up to it. The boat is undamaged, and I will sail as soon as I find replacements."*

- **Position and interest:** The captain is a supervisor writing to his employer. He wants to look in control and avoid blame.
- **Seen and unseen:** He saw the refusal itself. He did not see the crew's private conversations, so "the cook put them up to it" is a guess.
- **Word choice:** "Refused" frames it as defiance. "Put them up to it" suggests the crew couldn't act on their own.
- **Can show:** how a captain explained a disruption to the owner, and that the cook was named as a troublemaker. **Cannot show:** why the crew refused, or whether the cook had any role.

Notice that the "can't show" part isn't just "he might be biased." It names specific things the document has no access to.

**Why audience matters**
- **It shapes what gets included.** A writer picks details that serve the reader he's addressing. A report to a boss is built to be acted on and to protect the writer, not to give a full account.
- **It shapes what gets left out.** Anything that wouldn't help the writer with that reader tends to be missing or minimized.
- **It shapes the vocabulary.** The words are chosen for the reader. This is why the Reader asks you to notice "ringleaders" instead of "spokesmen."
- **It tells you what the document can't show.** If a letter was written to persuade a superior, it's weak evidence for what the people being described thought or wanted. It's much stronger evidence for how the writer wanted the situation to look.

**Back to the ferry example**

- Don't try to say everything. At 150 words, two or three well-supported points beat a long list.
