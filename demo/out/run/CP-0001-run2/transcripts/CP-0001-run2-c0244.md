2. **assistant**: That's a common sticking point, and it usually means the task is less "summarize the letter" than it looks. Unit 3 of the Primary Source Reader frames it as a two-column list written in prose:

1. **Position and interest:** who wrote it, to whom, and what they wanted that reader to believe or do.
2. **What they could see and what was out of view:** what the author's position gave them access to, and what it hid.
3. **Word choices:** places where the author picked one word over another available one.
4. **What the source can show vs. cannot show:** its evidence about the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, and who got named, versus things like the motives of the people described.

Here is the method on a parallel example, not your source. Imagine a mill owner's letter to his investors about a walkout, saying "agitators from outside misled my loyal hands." It shows how an owner wanted investors to see the strike, and that he blamed outsiders. It can't show why the workers actually walked out, because he wasn't in their meetings. "Agitators" and "loyal hands" are also chosen words, since he could have said "organizers" or "employees." A worker's diary of the same week would fill in what the letter can't.

Now for your worksheet, with the letter in front of you: whose perspective is missing from it, and what do you think that leaves the letter unable to show?
