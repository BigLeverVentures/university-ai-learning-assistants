**The steps from the Reader**
1. Name the author's position and interest: who is writing, to whom, and what they want.
2. Note what the author could see from that position and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available (the Reader's examples are "ringleaders" vs. "spokesmen" and "abandoned" vs. "left").
4. Say what the document can show (the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, the names recorded) and what it can't (the motives of the people described, what happened where the author wasn't).

**A worked example on a different, made-up source**

Suppose a landlord writes to a town council: *"The tenants on Mill Row have refused to pay since March, led by a troublemaker named Ames. Most are decent people who would pay if not intimidated. I request that the constable be sent."*

- **Position and interest:** The landlord wants the council to act, so he makes the problem sound like a few agitators versus a loyal majority.
- **Could see and couldn't:** He knows the rent ledger. He probably wasn't in the tenants' meetings, so his claim about "intimidation" is a guess.
- **Word choices:** "Troublemaker" and "intimidated" frame resistance as disorder, not as a response to rents or conditions.
- **Can show:** how an owner described the dispute, who he blamed by name, and what he asked authorities to do. **Can't show:** why the tenants stopped paying, or whether most of them actually disagreed with Ames.

In the worksheet, those four points get written as connected prose, not a list.
