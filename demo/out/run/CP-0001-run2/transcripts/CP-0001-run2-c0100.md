**The method, in four moves** (from Reader unit 3):
1. Name the author's position and interest.
2. Note what the author could see from there, and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available.
4. State what the document can show and what it cannot.

**Parallel example (invented, not your source):** a lock-keeper writes to the canal board in 1852 about a boatmen's walkout.

> "The boatmen have refused to move their craft since Tuesday. I believe most are decent men misled by two or three agitators, chiefly a hand named Corey. The lock is secure."

- **Position and interest:** He's an employee reporting to his employer, and he needs to look like he's in control.
- **Could see / couldn't see:** He could see the lock and who gathered nearby. He couldn't see the boatmen's private discussions or their reasons.
- **Word choice:** He says "agitators" where "organizers" or "spokesmen" were available, and "misled" where "persuaded" was available. Both words assume the men weren't acting on their own judgment.
- **Can show:** his view of the dispute, the language an employer would find reassuring, and one name he thought worth recording (Corey).
- **Cannot show:** why the boatmen stopped, whether most of them really were "misled," or what happened away from the lock.
