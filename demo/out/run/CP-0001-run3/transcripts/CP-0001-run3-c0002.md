1. **student**: I have to evaluate this letter from the foreman for my worksheet. I read it twice and I don't get what I'm supposed to say about it.
2. **assistant**: That's a common sticking point, and it usually means the task feels bigger than it is. Your worksheet asks for three things, and Reader unit 3 gives you a route to each:

1. **Perspective:** Who wrote it, to whom, and what did they want? (Author's position and interest.)
2. **What it can show:** What could the author see from where they stood? What does the document reveal, even unintentionally, through its word choices and what it records?
3. **What it cannot show:** What was out of the author's view, or what can't you know from this document alone?

Here's a worked example on a different, made-up source, so you can see the moves.

*Source: a harbor master's report to the town council after a storm, saying the "careless fishermen" ignored his warning flags and lost two boats.*

- **Perspective:** The harbor master is writing to the council, and his job may depend on looking competent. He has a reason to put the blame on others.
- **Can show:** His view of the event, and the council's likely expectations. The phrase "careless fishermen" shows how an official talked about working people. The report also records which boats were lost.
- **Cannot show:** Why the fishermen went out. Maybe the flags were not visible, or they needed the catch. It also can't show anything that happened out at sea, beyond his sight.

In prose, that becomes a few connected sentences that say what the source shows and what it doesn't, with a word or phrase as evidence.
