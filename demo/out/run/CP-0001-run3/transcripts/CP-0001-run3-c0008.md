**The method in four moves**
1. **Position and interest:** Who wrote it, to whom, and what did they want?
2. **Vantage point:** What could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choice:** Where did they pick one word when another was available?
4. **Can show / cannot show:** What does the document reveal (even unintentionally), and what can't it tell us?

**Worked example (invented, not your source)**
Imagine a ferry-company clerk's report to the owner after passengers refused to board during a fare increase:

*"A few troublemakers stood at the dock and turned people away. Most customers were willing to pay. I recorded the names of those I recognized."*

- **Position and interest:** He's an employee reporting to the owner, so he wants to look competent and loyal.
- **Vantage point:** He saw the dock, but not the conversations in people's homes or the reasons for refusing.
- **Word choice:** "Troublemakers" rather than "protesters." "Willing" is a claim about other people's minds.
- **Shows / can't show:** It shows how a clerk framed a protest for his boss and who he could name. It can't show why people refused, or whether "most" were really willing.

Notice the final step isn't "this source is biased, so it's useless." It's sorting what the source is good evidence *for* from what it isn't.
