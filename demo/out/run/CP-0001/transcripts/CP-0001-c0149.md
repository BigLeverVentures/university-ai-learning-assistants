**Practice example: B-3, G. Ambrose to the Thornbury town council, 1886**

**Provenance and perspective.** Ambrose is a landlord writing to the council, which has served a notice on his property. He is writing to get the notice withdrawn, so this is a defense, not a neutral description. He can see his rents, his costs, and his dealings with the council. He can't speak reliably for how his tenants live or why they act as they do, and he has every reason to present himself as reasonable.

**Evidence (what the words say):**
- He bought the court in 1879 and says it has returned "less than four per cent in any year."
- He says he has "twice paid to have [the drains] cleared."
- He claims tenants have put "families of nine in a single room against my express instruction."
- He offers a second standpipe "if the Council will withdraw the notice."

**Inference (what I'm reading into the evidence):**
- The standpipe offer reads as a bargaining move, not an admission of need. That's an inference from the "if" clause.
- Phrases like "the class of person who will live anywhere" suggest he assigns blame to tenants. That shows his attitude, but it doesn't show the tenants' behavior.
- His claims about costs, drains, and overcrowding are unverified. I'd treat them as his account, not as established fact.

**What it's useful for.** It shows how at least one landlord framed responsibility: tenants at fault, the council to blame for the lack of alternatives. It doesn't show the court's actual conditions, which I'd need another kind of source for.
