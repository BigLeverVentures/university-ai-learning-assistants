**Parallel example (invented): a detention book from Hollis Street School, March 1902.**
*Entries: Mar 3, Pike, talking, 1 hr. Mar 4, Pike, talking, 1 hr. Mar 5, Lowe, late, 30 min. Mar 10, Pike, impertinence, 2 hrs. Mar 17-21, no entries; note in margin: "school closed, roof repairs." Mar 24, Lowe, late, 30 min.*

**What the book was for.** The headteacher kept it to show that punishments were applied consistently, perhaps for inspectors or parents. So it records only what a teacher had to write down: date, name, offence, penalty.

**What it shows (evidence).** Talking and lateness were punishable offences. Pike appears three times in eight days. The penalty rose from 1 hr to 2 hrs between his second and third entries for different offences.

**What it suggests (inference, labeled as such).** Pike may have been singled out, or he may simply have been caught more often. The rising penalty *might* mean the school treated "impertinence" as more serious than "talking." The page can't confirm either.

**What it's silent about.** It doesn't show who talked without being caught, which offences a teacher let go, or what Pike thought about it. The empty week isn't a week of perfect behavior. The margin note tells us nobody was punished because nobody was there. That's a silence explained by other evidence.

Notice the three moves: purpose, what it shows, what it's silent about, kept apart. That's the structure Reader unit 5 asks for.
