## Practice example: B-3 (not your assigned document)

**1. Provenance and perspective**
- **Who and for whom:** G. Ambrose, a landlord, writing to the Thornbury town council in 1886. The signature "Esq." suggests a man of some standing (inference).
- **Purpose:** He's responding to a notice served on his property, so he's writing to get it withdrawn. That shapes everything in the letter.
- **What he could and couldn't see:** He owns the court, but there's no sign he lives there. His claims about tenants' behavior ("families of nine in a single room," tenants throwing refuse in the drains) are assertions, not observations he describes firsthand.

**2. Evidence vs. inference**
- *Evidence (what the text says):* He bought the court in 1879 and says it has returned "less than four per cent." He says the drains were "stopped" and that he "twice paid to have them cleared." He offers "a second standpipe" if the notice is withdrawn.
- *Inference (what I'd conclude from it):*
  - The drains' condition isn't disputed by him, only the cause. He doesn't deny the inspector's finding, he redirects blame (inference from "Your inspector found the drains stopped; they are stopped because...").
  - Offering a second standpipe suggests water supply was inadequate, though he never says so (inference).
  - The question "where the people are to go" suggests he sees enforcement as the Council's problem, not his (inference).

**3. What it's useful for, and its limits**
It shows how one landlord framed his responsibility and defended his interests. It can't tell us reliably what tenants did or how many people lived in each room, since those claims come from a party with a stake in the outcome.
