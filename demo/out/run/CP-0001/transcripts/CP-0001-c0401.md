**The method in brief**
1. **Position and interest:** Who wrote this, to whom, and what did they want the reader to believe or do?
2. **Sightlines:** What could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choices:** Where did the author pick one word when others were available? (The Reader's example is "ringleaders" vs. "spokesmen.")
4. **Can / cannot show:** What does the document reveal (the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, whose names were recorded), and what can't it reveal (other people's motives, events the author didn't witness)?

**Practice example (not your source)**
Imagine a mill owner's letter to his business partner: *"The hands walked out Tuesday, misled by agitators. Most are loyal and want to return."*
- **Position and interest:** An owner writing to a partner, so he wants to show he's in control and the loss isn't his fault.
- **Sightlines:** He saw the walkout from the office, but not the conversations among the workers.
- **Word choices:** "Agitators" and "misled" imply the workers couldn't act on their own judgment.
- **Can show:** How owners framed strikes, and what he wanted his partner to think.
- **Cannot show:** Why the workers actually left, or whether "most" wanted to return. That's his claim, not evidence.

Notice that the example's last point separates what the letter *says* from what we can *verify*. That distinction is the heart of your entry.
