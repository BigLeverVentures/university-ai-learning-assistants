6. **assistant**: Yes, it matters. Audience affects what a writer includes, what they stress, what they leave out, and what they risk if they get it wrong. It doesn't make a source false, but it tells you what pressures were on the writer.

Here's a parallel example, not from your packet. A mill manager writes to the company's owners during a walkout: "Production halted because outside agitators misled the workers." Years later, a weaver tells a local historian: "We stopped because wages were cut, and we kept the looms oiled ourselves." The manager is answering to his employers and may need to show he wasn't at fault or negligent. The weaver is speaking after the fact, to people who might remember it his way, and may want to defend his fellow workers' reputations. Both could be sincere, and both have reasons to frame events as they do. So the question isn't just "who's lying?" but "what could each see, and what did each want the reader to conclude?"

For your worksheet, this is the move the prompt wants: explain the disagreement through each author's position and purpose, rather than ruling on who is "right."
