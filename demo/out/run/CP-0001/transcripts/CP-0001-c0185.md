**The method (Reader unit 3)**
1. Name the author's position and interest: who is writing, to whom, and what do they want?
2. List what they could see from that position and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available.
4. State what the document can show (the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, the names recorded) and what it cannot (others' motives, events where the author wasn't present).

**Worked example (invented, not your source)**
Imagine a lock-keeper's note to a canal company: *"The boatmen refused to move their boats on Tuesday. I believe most were misled by a few troublemakers. I have kept the lock in good order."*

- **Position and interest:** A company employee reporting to his employer, who wants to look loyal and competent.
- **Could see / couldn't see:** He saw the boats sitting idle at his lock. He couldn't see what boatmen said among themselves or what happened at other locks.
- **Word choices:** "Refused" instead of "stopped," and "troublemakers" instead of "organizers." Both frame the boatmen as in the wrong.
- **Can show:** How a company man described a walkout, and that he thought "misled" was a safe explanation to give his boss. **Cannot show:** Why the boatmen actually stopped, or whether "most" of them really disagreed with the walkout.

Notice that each point comes from something specific in the text. Then the four moves get written up as a short paragraph rather than a list.

6. **assistant**: Yes, and it's the core of the method. The audience shapes what an author includes, what they leave out, and how they describe things. Reader unit 3 says this directly: a foreman's report "tells you about the strike, but it also tells you what a foreman thought his superintendent wanted to hear."

Use my lock-keeper example. Suppose he writes the same events in a letter to a fellow boatman instead of his company. He probably wouldn't call the boatmen "troublemakers," and he wouldn't stress how well he kept the lock. Same events, different account, because he's trying to do something different with each reader. So audience matters in two ways:

1. **It affects reliability.** You can't take the account as a neutral record, because it was built for a reader with power over the writer.
2. **It's evidence itself.** What the writer chose to say to that reader shows what he believed the reader valued: loyalty, order, competence.

That's why the "can show" column includes things like "the vocabulary of authority," not just facts about the strike.

For Pruitt, this means you can't treat his claims about the workers, such as how many wanted to strike, as simply true. They're claims made to his boss. You can still use them to see how he wanted the strike understood.

**Planning tips**
- Aim for roughly 40-50 words per part. 150 words goes fast, so pick your best evidence rather than covering everything.
- Quote or point to specific words or lines. "He was biased" is weak, while "he calls them 'ringleaders'" shows your reasoning.
- Write it as connected prose, not a list. The Reader says evaluating a source is the two-column list "written in prose."
