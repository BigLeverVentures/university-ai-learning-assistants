**The method**
1. **Name the author's position and interest.** Who is writing, to whom, and what do they want?
2. **What could the author see, and what was out of view?** Think about where they stood and who they knew.
3. **Notice word choices.** Look for places where other words were available, like the Reader's "ringleaders" vs. "spokesmen."
4. **State what the document can show and what it cannot.** It can show the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, and the names recorded. It cannot show the motives of the people described, or what happened where the author wasn't.

**A parallel example (invented, not your source)**
Imagine a letter from a ferry captain to the harbor board after a dockworkers' walkout: *"The crew deserted the pier at noon. I held the vessel with two loyal hands. The agitators are Cole and Tran."*

- *Position and interest:* A captain answering to the board, who wants to look in control and blameless.
- *Seen and unseen:* He saw the pier and his own deck. He did not hear what the crew discussed.
- *Word choice:* "Deserted" and "agitators" frame the walkout as disloyalty, where "left" or "organizers" would not.
- *Can and cannot show:* It shows how a captain talked to his board and which names he recorded. It can't show why the crew left or what Cole and Tran actually said.

Notice that each step gives you one or two sentences, and together they make a short paragraph.
