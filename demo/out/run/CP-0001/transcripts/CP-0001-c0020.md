6. **assistant**: Yes, it matters a lot, and it's the idea behind this whole unit. The Reader opens with it: "Every document was made by someone, for someone, to do something." A writer chooses what to include, what to stress, and what to leave out based on who will read it and what he wants from them.

Audience affects your evaluation in two ways:

1. **It shapes what the writer says.** If the reader has power over the writer, the writer has reasons to look loyal and competent, and to put blame elsewhere. That doesn't make the document false, but it means you can't take every claim at face value. It's why the Reader says a foreman's report "tells you about the strike, but it also tells you what a foreman thought his superintendent wanted to hear."

2. **It gives you evidence the writer didn't intend.** Reading against the grain means asking what the letter reveals *without meaning to*. A letter written for a boss shows you how someone below that boss spoke to him, and what he assumed the boss cared about.

In my ship captain example, the owners could fire the captain, so his account of the storm and the two crewmen has to be read with that in mind. You don't throw the account out, but you ask what it can and can't tell you.
