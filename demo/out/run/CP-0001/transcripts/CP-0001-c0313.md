**PRACTICE EXAMPLE (Set B, B-3, not for citation)**

**1. Provenance and perspective.**
- *Who/for whom/when:* G. Ambrose, a landlord, writing to the Thornbury town council in 1886, after a notice was served on his property.
- *Purpose:* He wants the notice withdrawn. He offers a second standpipe "if the Council will withdraw the notice," which makes the letter a negotiation, not a neutral description.
- *What he could and couldn't see:* He owns the court as "an investment for my daughters" and reports on tenants secondhand ("families of nine in a single room against my express instruction"). He doesn't say he lives there or visited. The tenants' voices are absent.

**2. Evidence vs. inference.**
- *Evidence (what the text says):* The court returned "less than four per cent"; it was built "before the present by-laws"; the drains are stopped; he says he paid to clear them twice.
- *Inference (mine):* The detail about returns suggests he sees the court mainly as a financial asset. His phrase "the class of person who will live anywhere" shows a view of the tenants, but it doesn't prove anything about their actual conditions.
- *Claims to treat cautiously:* That tenants block the drains is his explanation, offered while defending himself. The inspector's finding that the drains were stopped is the one point where the two perspectives overlap, and the cause is where they diverge.

**3. What the source is good for.**
It's strong evidence of how a landlord framed responsibility for housing conditions to the authorities. It's weak evidence of how people lived in the court. To learn that, you'd need to set it against other perspectives.

**4. Shape of an annotation (no text written).**
A two-sentence annotation typically does two things: says what the source is and who made it, then says what it's useful for and its limits.
