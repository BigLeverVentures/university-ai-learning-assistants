## Practice example: B-3 (not citable, not your assigned document)

**Provenance**
- **Who and for whom:** G. Ambrose, a landlord, writing to the Thornbury town council.
- **When and why:** 1886, in response to a notice served on his property in Tanner's Court. He is writing to contest or soften it.
- **What he could see:** He owns the court and says he has paid for drain clearing, so he has some firsthand knowledge of the property. He doesn't say he lives there or visits often.

**Evidence vs. inference**
- *Evidence:* He says he bought the court "as an investment for my daughters" and that it returned "less than four per cent." He says the tenants "throw refuse" into the drains. He offers "a second standpipe" if the notice is withdrawn.
- *Inference:* He is presenting himself as a modest, reluctant owner, not a profiteer. The standpipe offer reads as a bargaining move, since it's conditional on withdrawal. His claim about tenants and refuse is his explanation for the drains, not independent evidence of the cause.

**What the source is good for**
It shows how a landlord framed responsibility: blaming tenants, citing the age of the building, and asking who should pay. It tells us little about what residents themselves would say, and the inspector's account would be needed to test his claims.

**The moves to notice:** I identified the author, audience, and purpose first. Then I separated quoted words from my reading of them. Finally, I said what the source can and can't tell us.

## Practice example: B-3 (not citable, not your assigned document)

**Question: How does the landlord assign responsibility for conditions in Tanner's Court?**

> G. Ambrose's letter to the Thornbury council spreads responsibility across everyone but himself. He points first to the tenants, who "throw refuse" into the drains, and then to the town, which "did not require" higher standards when the court was built. He then turns the council's notice into a question about cost: if the court is to be closed, the council "should say where the people are to go and who is to pay." These claims are Ambrose's own. Nothing in the letter independently confirms the cause of the blocked drains or the court's history, and he wrote it to get the notice withdrawn. The letter is therefore better evidence of how a landlord defended his position than of what conditions were actually like.

**Moves to notice:** a claim in the first sentence, short quoted phrases as evidence, a clear break between what he says and what we can confirm, and a closing sentence about what the source is good for.
