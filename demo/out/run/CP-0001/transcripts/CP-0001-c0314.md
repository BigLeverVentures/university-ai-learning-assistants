## Practice example: B-3, G. Ambrose to Thornbury town council, 1886

**Provenance and perspective.** The author is a landlord, writing to the council in 1886 in response to a notice served on his property. He is defending himself. He had a direct financial stake in the outcome and could see his own accounts, but he doesn't live in the court and presumably doesn't see daily life there.

**Evidence (what the text says).**
- He bought Tanner's Court in 1879 and says it has returned "less than four per cent."
- He claims tenants put "families of nine in a single room against my express instruction."
- He says the drains are stopped "because the tenants throw refuse into them," and that he has twice paid to clear them.
- He offers a second standpipe if the notice is withdrawn.

**Inference (my reading, not stated in the text).**
- Calling the tenants "the class of person who will live anywhere" suggests he locates the cause of poor conditions in tenant behavior rather than in the building or his own management.
- The standpipe offer reads as a bargaining move, because it is conditional on the notice being withdrawn.
- "Say where the people are to go" suggests he sees closure as a threat to his income, and perhaps he is also pointing to a real shortage of housing.

**Silences.** There are no tenant voices. There are no figures for rents, repairs, or the number of occupants beyond his claim about "families of nine." We also only have his account of the inspector's findings.

**Takeaway.** The letter tells us much more about how a landlord justified himself to officials than about conditions in the court. As evidence of conditions, it needs checking against other sources.
