2. **assistant**: A list can show a lot, but only if you read it in a particular way. Your Reader unit 5 gives the method: administrative records weren't written to tell a story. They record what a clerk was required to write down, and that's why they're useful. You read them in two directions:

- **Down the page:** who appears, how often, and for what?
- **Across time:** where do entries thin out or stop, and what else was happening then?

Here's a quick **practice example on invented material, not your assignment**. Imagine a school detention book:

*Mar 3, J. Lee, late. Mar 4, J. Lee, late. Mar 5, R. Cho, talking. Mar 6, J. Lee, late. Mar 9-13, no entries; margin: "school closed, snow."*

- **Down:** Lee appears three times, always for lateness. That suggests the school enforced punctuality and that Lee was caught repeatedly. It does *not* tell us Lee was the only one who was ever late.
- **Across:** The blank week isn't evidence of perfect behavior. The margin note explains it. Nobody was fined because nobody was there.
- **Silence:** The book can't tell us what Lee thought, which offences teachers let slide, or why Lee was late.

Notice the three kinds of statements: what the record was for, what it shows, and what it's silent about. Your response should keep them apart, with evidence (the actual entries) separate from your inference.

So a list of names and amounts isn't empty. It's a trace of which rules were enforced, against whom, and when. Now back to your page, with one question to start: **who wrote this ledger and for what purpose, and whose perspective is therefore missing from it?**
