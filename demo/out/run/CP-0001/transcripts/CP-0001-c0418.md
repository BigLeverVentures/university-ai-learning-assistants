6. **assistant**: Yes, it matters a lot, and the Reader's central idea is built on it: "Every document was made by someone, for someone, to do something."

Audience shapes three things:

- **What gets included.** A writer picks details that serve the reader's needs and his own. A report to a boss is selective in a way a private diary or a letter to a friend wouldn't be.
- **How things are described.** The same event can be told differently depending on who is reading and what the writer wants from them. That's why the Reader pays attention to word choices like "ringleaders" instead of "spokesmen."
- **What the document is evidence of.** When the writer is reporting up to someone with power over him, the letter tells you about the strike, and it also tells you what the writer thought that reader wanted to hear. The Reader says this directly in its foreman example.

So audience affects how much weight you can put on each claim. A statement like "the engines are safe" and a statement like "I do not believe one man in five wished to strike" may not deserve the same trust. One is something Pruitt could check himself, and the other is a judgment about people's minds.

Look at those two statements in the letter. Which one do you think Pruitt was in a better position to know, and why?
