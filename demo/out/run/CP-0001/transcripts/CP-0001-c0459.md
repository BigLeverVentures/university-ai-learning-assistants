**What a list can show: a parallel example (invented, not your document)**
Imagine a school's detention book kept by an office clerk:

> Mon: Lee, late, 10 min. Tue: Park, phone in class. Wed: Lee, late. Thu: *(no entries)*. Fri: Lee, late.

Even this tiny list tells us a few things:
- **The rules:** lateness and phones were punishable, so the school enforced them.
- **Who and how often:** Lee appears three times, so either Lee was often late or Lee was watched closely. That is an inference, not something the book states.
- **The gap:** Thursday is blank. That could mean perfect behavior, but it could also mean a snow day. A blank is a fact to be explained.

What the book can't tell us is who broke the rules without being noticed, who got let off, or what Lee thought about it.

**How this maps to your task**
The Reader suggests reading a ledger in two directions: down the page (who, how often, for what) and across time (where entries thin out or stop, and what else might have been going on). A good response also keeps three things separate: what the record was for, what it shows about discipline, and what it's silent about.
