2. **assistant**: That's a common stumbling block, and it usually means the task is different from what you expected. You aren't being asked to summarize the letter or decide whether Pruitt is right. Reader unit 3 asks you to treat the letter as evidence about *Pruitt* and what he could see, as well as about the strike.

The method has four steps:
1. Name the author's position and interest.
2. List what he could see from there and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available.
4. State what the source can show and what it cannot.

Here's a worked example on a made-up source, so you can see the moves.

**Parallel source (invented):** A harbor master writes to the harbor board: "The fishermen ignored my storm flags and went out recklessly. The boats that were lost were poorly kept. I did all that my post allowed."

- **Position and interest:** He's an official reporting upward after a disaster, so he has reason to show he did his job.
- **Could see / out of view:** He could see the flags, the departures, and the harbor. He couldn't see the fishermen's reasons, such as whether they needed the catch or could even see the flags.
- **Word choice:** "Recklessly" and "poorly kept" are judgments, where "went out" and "older boats" would be plain description.
- **Can show:** How an official explained a loss and protected himself, and what he thought the board would accept.
- **Cannot show:** Why the fishermen sailed, or whether the warning was clear.

Then you'd turn those notes into a few connected sentences of prose. The Reader calls it a two-column list written as prose.
