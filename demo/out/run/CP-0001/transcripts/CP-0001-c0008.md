**The method, in four moves**
1. **Author's position and interest:** Who wrote it, to whom, and what did they want or need from the reader?
2. **Vantage point:** What could the author see from where they stood, and what was out of view?
3. **Word choices:** Where did the author use a loaded word when a neutral one was available?
4. **Can show / cannot show:** What does the document reveal (including things the author didn't mean to reveal), and what can't it tell us?

Your 150 words are basically that two-column list written as prose: what the source can show, and what it can't.

**Worked example on parallel material (not your source)**
Imagine a lighthouse keeper's report to his district inspector after a ship ran aground on his coast. He writes that the "reckless" captain ignored the light, and that he himself kept the lamp burning all night.

- *Position and interest:* He's a subordinate reporting to a superior, and he has reason to show he did his job.
- *Vantage:* He saw the light and the wreck from shore. He couldn't see the ship's bridge or hear the captain's decisions.
- *Word choice:* "Reckless" is a judgment. "Ship that came too close" would have been a description.
- *Can show:* How a keeper presented his own diligence, and what he assumed the inspector wanted to hear. *Cannot show:* why the captain steered as he did.

Notice that the example doesn't say "the keeper is biased, so it's useless." It says what kind of evidence this is.
