**The steps (from Reader unit 3):**
1. Name the author's position and interest.
2. List what the author could see from that position and what was out of view.
3. Notice word choices where other words were available.
4. State what the document can show and what it cannot.

**A worked example on a different source (invented, not your assignment):**
Suppose a schoolmaster writes to the school board in 1880: *"Attendance has fallen to nine of thirty because the farm families keep their children home for the harvest, as they always do. I have sent notes to the parents, but none have replied."*

- **Position and interest:** He's a employee reporting to his employers. He likely wants to show he is doing his job and isn't to blame for low attendance.
- **Could see / couldn't see:** He could see who was in the room and who wasn't. He couldn't see what happened in the farm households or why parents didn't reply. Maybe they couldn't read, or they disagreed with him.
- **Word choice:** "Keep their children home" puts the decision on the parents. "Children needed in the fields" would have framed it as a necessity.
- **Can show:** the attendance pattern as he counted it, how he explained it to the board, and what he assumed the board would accept. **Cannot show:** the parents' reasons or the children's own experience.

The Reader's advice is to write all this as a short prose evaluation, not as two lists.

6. **assistant**: Yes, audience matters a lot. It's one of the main ideas in unit 3. The Reader's opening line says every document is made "by someone, for someone, to do something." The "for someone" part shapes what gets included, what gets left out, and how things are worded.

**Why it matters:**
- **It shapes what the author says.** People write differently to a boss than to a friend or a newspaper. A writer who answers to a superior tends to stress what makes them look capable and loyal, and to play down what doesn't.
- **It shapes the vocabulary.** The Reader points to word choices like "ringleaders" instead of "spokesmen." Words like these often reflect what the audience expects to hear.
- **It tells you what the document can show.** Because of the audience, the document is good evidence of how the author wanted the situation understood by that reader. It is weaker evidence of what everyone involved actually thought or did.

**A parallel example (not your source):** Imagine a ship's captain writes two accounts of a failed voyage, one to the ship's owners and one in a private letter to his brother. In the first he may blame the weather and the crew. In the second he may admit he misjudged the route. Neither account is useless, but each shows something different, and the difference comes from the audience.
