**Practice example: B-3, letter from G. Ambrose to the Thornbury town council, 1886**

**Provenance and perspective.** Ambrose is a property owner writing to the council that served a notice on his property. He wrote in 1886 to persuade, so his aim is to get the notice withdrawn. He could see his own accounts and his dealings with his agents. He could not see inside the tenants' daily lives, and he has every reason to present himself favorably.

**What the source says (evidence).**
- He bought Tanner's Court in 1879 "as an investment for my daughters" and says it has returned "less than four per cent."
- He says the court predates the by-laws.
- He says the drains are stopped because "the tenants throw refuse into them," and that he has "twice paid to have them cleared."
- He asks where displaced people are "to go and who is to pay."
- He offers a second standpipe if the notice is withdrawn.

**Interpretation (inference).**
- The mention of daughters and low returns suggests he is framing himself as a modest, even vulnerable owner rather than a speculator. This is inference, since the letter doesn't say how he wants to be seen.
- Blaming tenants for the drains shifts responsibility away from the owner. The letter gives no independent confirmation of either his account or the inspector's.
- The standpipe offer is conditional on withdrawal, which reads as a bargaining move rather than a repair commitment.
- His question about where people will go points to a real problem, but he uses it as an argument against enforcement.

**What it can and can't tell us.** It shows how one landlord justified himself to officials. It doesn't establish the actual condition of the court or what tenants thought.

**Sample annotation (two sentences).** This 1886 letter from landlord G. Ambrose to the Thornbury council defends his property against a sanitary notice by blaming tenants and citing poor returns. It is useful for seeing an owner's self-justification, but it is a partisan source with no tenant voice.
