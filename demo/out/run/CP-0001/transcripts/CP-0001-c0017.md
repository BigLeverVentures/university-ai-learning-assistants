2. **assistant**: That's a common stuck point, and it helps to know the worksheet isn't asking for a summary of the letter. It asks you to treat the letter as evidence, using the two-column method in Reader unit 3.

**The method in four moves**
1. **Author's position and interest:** Who is writing, to whom, and what does he want from that reader?
2. **Vantage point:** What could he see from where he stood, and what was out of his view?
3. **Word choices:** Where did he pick one word when others were available?
4. **Can show / cannot show:** What does the letter reveal (his view, the language of authority, the names he recorded), and what can't it tell you (other people's motives, events he didn't witness)?

**A worked example on invented material (not your source)**
Imagine a note from a mill overseer to the owner: *"The weavers walked out Tuesday. A few troublemakers stirred up the rest. I kept the looms oiled and await your orders."*

- *Position and interest:* The overseer answers to the owner and wants to look competent and loyal.
- *Vantage:* He saw the walkout itself and the oiled looms. He did not hear what the weavers said to each other, or why.
- *Word choice:* "Troublemakers" casts the leaders as a problem, where "organizers" would have framed them differently.
- *Can show:* How an overseer described a walkout to his boss, and what he thought the owner wanted to hear. *Cannot show:* why the weavers left, or whether "a few" is accurate.

In prose, that becomes your two columns written as sentences.

**Why audience matters**
- **It shapes what gets included.** A writer picks details that serve his purpose with that particular reader. A report to a boss will stress different things than a letter to a friend or a speech to coworkers.
- **It shapes the wording.** People choose words their reader will accept. That's why the Reader has you notice word choices where other words were available.
- **It tells you what the document can show.** If Pruitt wrote to please his superior, the letter is strong evidence of how a foreman presented events to authority. It's weaker evidence of what the workers actually thought or wanted.

**Back to the mill example (not your source):** the overseer wrote "a few troublemakers stirred up the rest" to an owner.
