1. student: I have to evaluate this letter from the foreman for my worksheet. I read it twice and I don't get what I'm supposed to say about it.
2. assistant: That's a common sticking point, and the worksheet is asking for something different from a summary. Unit 3 of the Reader frames it as a two-column list written in prose: 1. **Position and interest:** who made the document, for whom, and what they wanted the reader to believe or do. 2. **What they could and couldn't see** from where they stood. 3. **Word choices** where other words were available. 4. **What the source can show** (the author's view, the vocabulary of authority, the names recorded) and **what it cannot show** (other people's motives, events outside the author's view). Here's the method on a parallel example, not your letter. Imagine a mill owner writes to the town council: "A mob of agitators shut down my mill, though most of my loyal hands wanted to work." - **Position and interest:** he's an owner asking the council for help, so he wants to look like the victim of outsiders. - **Word choices:** he says "mob" and "agitators" where "crowd" or "organizers" were available. - **Can show:** how owners described strikers, and what he hoped officials would accept. - **Cannot show:** why the workers actually walked out, or whether "most wanted to work" is true. He can't see into their minds, and he has a reason to say it. Your entry should do the same kind of work with Pruitt's letter, in about 150 words and your own phrasing. To get you started, one question: looking at the top and bottom of the letter, who is Pruitt writing to, and what does he seem to need from that person?
