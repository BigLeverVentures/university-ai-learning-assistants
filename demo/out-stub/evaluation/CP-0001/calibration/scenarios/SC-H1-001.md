## Source: Course packet excerpt A: Carrow Junction Evening Dispatch, editorial, 26 July 1877 (invented)

THE STRIKE AND ITS AUTHORS.—The stoppage at the Carrow Junction shops, now entering its fourth day, has been represented in certain quarters as the spontaneous act of our working men. We do not believe it. The men of this town have had steady work at fair wages for the better part of a decade, and until Monday last we never heard a murmur against the Company. That they should down tools within an hour of the arrival of the eastern train, and that strangers were seen at the roundhouse gate addressing the shopmen before the whistle had finished, is to our mind a plain story: the strikers were misled by outside agitators who have nothing to lose here and will be gone before the first pay is missed. We counsel the men to return. The Company has given its word that no man who resumes work on Monday will be marked for it. Those who refuse will have only themselves, and their new friends, to thank for what follows. Trade in the town is already suffering; the grocers report accounts going unpaid. Let the men consider who it is that bears the cost.

## Source: Course packet excerpt B: notice posted at the Carrow Junction depot by the Shopmen's Committee, 25 July 1877 (invented)

TO THE PUBLIC OF CARROW JUNCTION. The men of the machine shop, roundhouse, and car shop have stopped work. We did not stop for strangers and we did not stop for sport. On the first of this month the Company cut wages ten per cent for the second time in a year, and on the twentieth it posted that the shops would run nine hours for ten hours' pay. We asked to meet the Superintendent and were refused. We ask the public to know three things. First, no man has been threatened by this Committee, and no property has been touched. Second, we will protect the Company's engines from harm as we always have. Third, we will return the day the July cut is restored and the Superintendent agrees to hear a committee of the men. Signed for the Committee: J. Halloran, brakeman; T. Reese, machinist; A. Dunmore, carpenter.

## Source: Course packet excerpt C: letter from shop foreman E. Pruitt to the division superintendent, 28 July 1877 (invented)

Sir,—I have the honor to report on the condition of the shops since Monday the 23rd. The men came in at the usual hour and worked until about eleven, when word came that the eastern freights had stopped at Marlow. A number of men left their benches to talk at the roundhouse gate, where two persons not in the Company's employ were speaking to them. By noon the shops were abandoned. I kept the fires banked with the help of the three watchmen and have done so since. I do not believe one man in five wished to strike. The ringleaders are Halloran, a brakeman who has no business in the shops at all, Reese of the machine shop, and Dunmore of the car shop; they have held the men together by meetings at the depot each evening. I am told the quieter men would return if assured against discharge. I have given no such assurance, having no authority to. The engines are safe. I will continue to report daily and await your instructions as to the watchmen's pay, which is now owing for the week. Your obedient servant, E. Pruitt, Foreman.

## Source: Course packet excerpt F: from the memoir of former brakeman J. Halloran, published 1902 (invented)

Twenty-five years have gone since that July, and most of the men I stood with are dead. I have read since, in a book got up from the Company's papers, that the shops were abandoned by noon and that strangers put us up to it. Whoever wrote that was not at the roundhouse. We held the shops till the militia came on the Thursday. Reese kept the fires himself so that no man could say we let the engines spoil, and we posted our own watch at the gate. The two men the foreman called strangers were Dunmore's brother and a boilermaker from Marlow who had walked the line to tell us the freights had stopped there. I was not in the shops that morning; I was a brakeman and had no bench to leave. But I was at the depot every evening after, and I will say plainly that the men did not need me to tell them what a ten per cent cut meant. The committee was chosen by a show of hands. I have the paper still. When I am gone it should go to the town, not to the Company.

## Source: Wrenfield Primary Source Reader, unit 6: From analysis to argument (invented)

An argument about sources is a claim about how to read them, supported by what the sources show. A thesis that could be true of any strike anywhere ("the newspapers were biased") is too broad to argue; it names no mechanism, no group, no comparison. Narrow it by asking: biased how, toward whom, compared with which other account, and with what effect on who was believed? A workable thesis for Essay 2 names the accounts it compares, the difference it has found between them, and the explanation it proposes for the difference. The evidence is then the specific passages that show the difference. Each paragraph should put a passage on the table, say what it shows, and say what it cannot show before moving on. Avoid summarizing the strike; your reader has the packet. Spend your words on the comparison. The conclusion should say what a reader of these accounts is entitled to believe and what remains unknown, which is also where the next question lives.
